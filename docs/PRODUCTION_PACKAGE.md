|---|---|
| Each chapter opens on one named place right away. **How this film is different:** four places instead of 24. One argument runs through all of them: *mapped → observed → sampled → described*. Each chapter answers the question the previous one raised. Every number is sourced and dated. Where something is unknown, the film says plainly what is unknown and why.

## 1. Research dossier: verified locations

The candidate list was checked and **Lake Vostok was replaced with Subglacial Lake Whillans.** The reason: Vostok was only ever reached by a borehole that used kerosene-based drilling fluid, and the microbial results from it are disputed. Whillans was sampled in 2013 with a clean hot-water drill, and the results were published in *Nature*. It is the better-documented case.

## 4. Narration, ready to record (1,065 words)

*Source of truth: `video/src/data/narration.json`. Paragraph breaks are breath points, and the edit and captions are timed to them. Target pace 150–155 wpm, which gives about 7:05 of voice.*

**[OPEN: THE MAP]**
Open a world map and Earth looks finished. Every coastline is drawn. Every ocean has a name. Even the floor of the Pacific has ridges and mountains on it.

But most of that seafloor has never been mapped in detail. Much of its shape is estimated from satellites, which detect tiny bumps in the height of the sea surface. As of April 2026, about twenty-nine percent of the ocean floor had been mapped to modern standards.

And the part anyone has actually seen with a camera is far smaller. A 2025 study put it at less than a thousandth of one percent of the deep seafloor. Every look since 1958, combined, covers an area about a tenth the size of Belgium.

So a map can be complete and still say very little about what is there. Water, ice, rock and forest each hide that gap in a different way. Follow all four, and this same map will start to look very different.

**[TITLE CARD, no voice]**

**[01 · WATER]**
Start in the Pacific, between Hawaii and Mexico. The Clarion-Clipperton Zone is a muddy abyssal plain of roughly six million square kilometres, on average about five kilometres down.

The barrier here is the water itself. Sunlight fades out within the top thousand metres. At five thousand, the pressure is roughly five hundred times what you feel at the surface.

So scientists work at the end of a cable. Ship-mounted sonar maps the plain in long swaths. Remotely operated vehicles film the seafloor and collect animals. Box corers bring up squares of mud.

By deep-sea standards, this is well-studied ground. Expeditions have come here since the 1960s. In 2023, researchers at London's Natural History Museum combined more than a hundred thousand records into one checklist. They counted 5,578 species. Roughly nine in ten had no scientific name.

That is the gap in its clearest form. These animals were found, photographed and preserved, yet most have never been formally described. How they live, and how far they range, is largely unknown. And that matters, because parts of this plain are being explored for mining.

The ocean at least lets sound and cables through. What happens when the barrier is solid?

**[02 · ICE]**
West Antarctica. The Whillans Ice Stream looks like an endless, flat sheet of snow. Nothing on the surface marks the lake beneath it.

It was found from orbit. In the 2000s, NASA's ICESat satellite measured the ice surface here rising and falling as water filled and drained below. Radar and seismic surveys then traced the lake's outline. But an outline is not a sample.

In January 2013, the WISSARD project melted a borehole about sixty centimetres wide through roughly eight hundred metres of ice, using a hot-water drill designed to keep surface microbes out.

At the bottom, a camera found the lake. At the borehole, it was only about two metres deep. And it was inhabited: around one hundred and thirty thousand microbial cells in every millilitre of water, and at least 3,931 distinct microbial types identified by their DNA. Many draw energy from chemicals, not sunlight.

A 2022 inventory lists 675 subglacial lakes in Antarctica. Only a handful have ever been sampled directly. One borehole describes one point in one lake. How these waters connect, and how their life varies, is still largely open.

A drill can reach a lake. But some spaces can only be entered on foot.

**[03 · ROCK]**
Beneath Carlsbad Caverns National Park in New Mexico, Lechuguilla Cave was long considered minor: a deep entrance pit and a few hundred feet of dead-end passage. But in the 1950s, cavers heard wind roaring up through the rubble on its floor. Moving air meant open space somewhere below.

Cavers began digging, with park permission, in 1984. On May 26, 1986, they broke through into large walking passages.

No satellite can map this. Every line on Lechuguilla's map was measured by people: distance, direction and slope from one survey point to the next, sketched by hand, often while crawling.

Since 1984, those teams have mapped more than 145 miles of passage, about 233 kilometres, reaching 489 metres deep. The cave was dissolved from below by sulfuric acid, and it holds gypsum chandeliers six metres long.

It also holds microbes. In a section isolated from the surface for over four million years, researchers found bacteria already resistant to antibiotics, some to fourteen different commercial drugs. Resistance, it turns out, is ancient.

The map ends where the survey ends. Past the last measured point there may be more passage, or none. Survey teams still go back to find out.

Underground, nobody can see from above. In a forest, we can. So how can a forest still surprise scientists?

**[04 · FOREST]**
In 2005, scientists working with Kew Gardens were scanning Google Earth for highland forests in Mozambique when they noticed a dark green patch on a mountain called Mabu.

From above, a canopy is a surface. Satellite images show its extent, its texture, its edges. They cannot show a chameleon on a twig, or what lives beneath trees forty to fifty metres tall.

So researchers walked in: a first visit in December 2005, then a larger Kew-led expedition in 2008 with Mozambican, Malawian and international partners. They documented what is probably the largest medium-altitude rainforest in southern Africa, some seventy to eighty square kilometres, and animals new to science: the Mabu forest viper, a pygmy chameleon, a horseshoe bat, new butterflies. By 2016, thirteen new species had been recorded.

But Mabu was never a lost forest. It was unknown to biologists, not to people. Local hunters knew its animals and helped researchers record them. During Mozambique's civil war, families reportedly sheltered inside the forest.

Even so, most surveys have stayed near the eastern edge and the summit. As of 2016, the drier western and northern slopes had not been visited by scientists.

**[RETURN]**
Back to the map. Nothing on it has moved. The Pacific, Antarctica, New Mexico and Mozambique are exactly where they were.

But now it reads in layers. Seafloor shapes estimated from satellites. Swaths measured by sonar. Narrow tracks where a camera passed. A borehole sixty centimetres wide. Passages measured by hand. Forest edges walked by scientists, inside landscapes long known to the people who live there.

A map tells us where things are. It rarely tells us how we know. Earth isn't unexplored. It is unevenly observed. And the most interesting questions still begin at the last measured point.

**Recording notes:** Pronounce *Lechuguilla* "letch-oo-GHEE-ya", *Whillans* "WILL-uns", *Mabu* "MAH-boo", *Clarion-Clipperton* "CLAIR-ee-un CLIP-er-tun". Say "5,578" as "five thousand, five hundred and seventy-eight". Say "3,931" as "three thousand, nine hundred and thirty-one". Hold a half-beat before "But an outline is not a sample", "But Mabu was never a lost forest" and "Earth isn't unexplored". Avoid any "mystery" inflection: these are findings, not teasers.

## 8. Remotion implementation (built: `video/`)

**Format:** 854 × 480, 30 fps, H.264 CRF 23, AAC 160 kb/s. Total 13,262 frames (7:22.1).

**Structure**
```
video/
  src/data/narration.json   ← script, split into scenes and paragraphs (single source of truth)
  src/data/timing.json      ← generated: scene lengths, paragraph start times, caption cues
  src/theme.ts              ← palette tokens, Inter type scale, SAFE_BOTTOM = 400
  src/lib/anim.ts           ← one easing curve (bezier .45,0,.2,1), ramp/window helpers
  src/lib/timing.ts         ← paraFrame(scene, i, offset): every visual beat is keyed to narration
  src/lib/geo.ts            ← Natural Earth land/borders + the four coordinates
  src/components/           ← Captions, Grain/Vignette, ChapterTag, Label, Stat, Credit
  src/scenes/               ← MapReveal, Descent, IceCutaway, CaveSurvey, ForestView, FinalReturn, TitleCard
  tools/tts.sh              ← scratch narration (Gemini TTS)
  tools/build_timing.py     ← silence detection → paragraph/sentence timing
  tools/ambience.sh         ← designed ambience beds, normalised to about −40 LUFS
  tools/stt_check.sh        ← transcribes the VO and diffs it against the script
  tools/stills.mjs          ← QA stills at chosen frames
```

**Timing model.** Each scene is 0.8 s lead-in + VO length + 1.4 s tail. `build_timing.py` runs `ffmpeg silencedetect` on each VO file. A monotone dynamic-programming pass snaps every sentence boundary to the nearest real pause, so captions and paragraph-keyed visuals follow the actual read. This is not forced alignment: re-run it after recording a new VO and spot-check in Studio.

**Reusable scenes.** `Globe` (orthographic, props for rotation, radius, centre, stipple and dim) and `FlatMap` (Equal Earth with markers) are shared by the opening, the Descent zoom and the final return. That is how the ending visibly reuses the opening map.

**Transitions.** Only crossfades and motivated match cuts: sonar rings → radar pulses (Water → Ice) and borehole line → survey line (Ice → Rock). There are no whip-pans and no glitch effects.

**Typography.** Inter (system font in the render container). Kicker 11 px/600/2.2 tracking. Labels 11–13 px/500. Stats 26–44 px/700. Title 34 px/700. Amber (#E8A33D) appears only for key measurements and uncertainty.

**Captions.** Burned in, 17 px, in a navy panel in the bottom band, one sentence or half-sentence per cue. **For YouTube, also upload an SRT generated from `timing.json`**, so captions can be switched off.

**Grain.** Film grain is added in a post pass (`ffmpeg -vf noise=alls=5:allf=t`), not in React. A per-frame SVG `feTurbulence` grain made the render about 8× slower (1.6 fps vs 13 fps). The vignette stays in React.

**Asset handling.** Everything lives in `public/` and loads with `staticFile()`. Images are pre-sized to ≤ 1280 px. Credits render on screen whenever an asset is visible.


## 11. Final quality review

**Repetition.**
- "Map" carries the motif and is used deliberately.
- "Largely unknown" and "largely open" appear once each, in different chapters. That is acceptable, but the human VO read could change one to "still open".
- No chapter repeats another's structure exactly: each one introduces a different method (sonar, drill, hand survey, walking).

**Transitions.**
- Water → Ice uses a question plus the sonar-to-radar match cut. Strong.
- Ice → Rock ("some spaces can only be entered on foot") and Rock → Forest ("in a forest, we can") both answer each other. Good.
- Forest → Return is the weakest. It ends on a dated fact with no bridge line. *Fix option:* add "That gap, between seeing a place and knowing it, runs through all four." (+4 s).

**Readability.**
- At 480p, 9.5–10.5 px credit and footnote text is at the limit of legibility. It is legible in the stills, but on a phone it will be soft. For a 1080p master, scale all `T` sizes by 2.25 rather than up-scaling this render.
- The 4 px "seen" square is intentionally tiny. It has a leader line and a label, so it doesn't rely on being spotted.

**Unsupported claims.**
- None were found in the narration after the audit.
- One on-screen value (lake ≈ 59 km²) rests on a secondary source and is flagged above.
- The ice-surface curve and the dashed cave extension are labelled illustrative on screen.

**Sound.**
- All ambience is designed (filtered noise, sine pings) and sits about 20 dB under the narration. None of it is presented as a recording.
- No music.
- The scratch narration uses three Gemini TTS model versions, because of free-tier quota limits, so the timbre shifts slightly between chapters. **Replace it with a single human read.**

**Production feasibility.**
- Everything in the cut is built from verified-licence imagery plus vectors, so it can be published once A4 (share-alike) and A7 (synthetic voice) are resolved.
- The suggested NOAA, USAP and Kew footage (A9–A11) would improve immersion. Insert them without changing timing: each slot is a full-frame image window in an existing scene.

**Meaning without audio.**
- Every key number appears on screen with its date or source.
- The chapter tags carry the structure.
- The burned-in captions carry the argument.
