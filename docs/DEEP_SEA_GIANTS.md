## Facts and sources (checked 7 Nov 2026)
| Claim | Source |
|---|---|

| Adults up to ~7 m and ~500 kg; believed to have the largest eyes of any animal | Discover Wildlife / Schmidt Ocean Institute (sizes are estimates) |
| Japanese spider crab: up to 12.5 ft (3.8 m) claw to claw; largest crab | National Geographic |
| Giant oarfish ~8 m; longest bony fish; seen swimming upright | Wikipedia (citing McClain et al. 2015); Florida Museum (Guinness: longest bony fish) |
| Giant *Apolemia* siphonophore, Ningaloo Canyons, April 2020; ring ≈ 45–47 m | Schmidt Ocean Institute; The Guardian (2022); siphonophores are colonial organisms |
| Below 1,000 m there's no sunlight; marine snow is the main food | NOAA; deep-sea literature |
| Greenland shark: >6 m, grows <1 cm a year; the largest of 28 studied (5 m) dated 272–512 years; longest-lived vertebrate | NOAA Ocean Service (updated 23 Sep 2026); Nielsen et al. 2016, *Science* |
| Giant isopod up to ~50 cm; Toba Aquarium "No.1" went 5 years 43 days without food (last meal 2 Jan 2009, died 14 Feb 2014) | Toba Aquarium (via BlueHub Lab summary); Japan Times 2013; NPR 2014 |
| Four leading hypotheses for gigantism: cold, oxygen, food scarcity (Kleiber's law), low predation and long life; no single answer | Wikipedia "Deep-sea gigantism"; Chapelle & Peck 1999, *Nature* (1,853 amphipod species) |
| Most deep-sea benthic biomass is tiny (<1 mm); gigantism is limited to certain groups | BlueHub Lab review of the literature |

| TMAO limit ≈ 8,200–8,400 m | NHM (quoting researchers); Yancey et al. 2014 *PNAS* prediction, cited in the 2023 paper |

| < 0.001% of the deep seafloor visually observed | Bell et al. 2025, *Science Advances* |
