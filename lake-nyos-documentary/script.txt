On the morning of August twenty-second, 1986, a man named Ephriam Che walked down to the lake below his home in northwest Cameroon.

The water, normally clear blue, had turned a dull red. The waterfall at the lake's outlet was dry. And there was no sound. No birds. No insects.

In the valleys around the lake, at least seventeen hundred people were dead. None of them had drowned. The lake had killed them through the air.

Lake Nyos sits high in the grasslands, in a volcanic crater only a few hundred years old. It's more than two hundred metres deep, and the farmland around it is rich.

At about half past nine on the night of August twenty-first, people near the lake heard a rumbling. It lasted perhaps fifteen or twenty seconds.

Ephriam Che thought it sounded like a rockslide. He saw a white mist rise from the water, assumed rain was coming, and went to bed feeling ill.

Halima Suley, who lived nearby, heard what sounded like "the shouting of many voices." Then a strong wind swept through her family's compound, and she collapsed, "like a dead person."

When she woke, her children were dead. So were thirty-one other members of her family, and four hundred of their cattle.

Survivors woke six to thirty-six hours later, weak and confused. In Nyos village, most people never woke. That morning, Che found Suley among her dead. "On that day," he said, "there were no flies on the dead." The flies had died too.

Scientists arrived within days. Their first suspect was the volcano.

But there was no lava, no fresh ash, and no reported earthquake. The water wasn't hot. And the lake floor showed no sign of being blown open.

Many survivors had skin sores and believed the gas had burned them. Doctors concluded most of those wounds came from lying motionless for hours. Tests found no evidence of cyanide or carbon monoxide.

There had been one warning. In 1984, thirty-seven people died on a low stretch of road beside Lake Monoun, about a hundred kilometres to the south. No eruption was found there either.

The lake held the rest of the answer. Its surface was covered in floating vegetation. A surge had washed twenty-five metres up the southern shore, and water had been thrown over a rocky point eighty metres high.

Water pulled up from the depths fizzed as it reached the surface. It was loaded with carbon dioxide.

The gas hadn't come from an eruption that night. The lake had been holding it. And on August twenty-first, it let go.

About eighty kilometres beneath the region, there is magma. Carbon dioxide from it rises slowly through the crust, dissolves into groundwater, and seeps into the bottom of Lake Nyos.

In most lakes, that gas escapes as the water mixes. But Lake Nyos is deep, sheltered, and layered. The bottom water, heavy with dissolved gas, is denser than the water above it, so it stays down.

Two hundred metres of water press on it with about twenty times the force of the atmosphere. Under that pressure, water can hold an enormous amount of gas.

It's like a sealed bottle of sparkling water. Cap on, no bubbles. Open it, and the gas comes out. At Nyos, the cap was the weight of the lake itself.

Now imagine some of that deep water is pushed upward. As it rises, the pressure falls and bubbles form. The bubbles make the water lighter, so it rises faster and releases more gas. Once it starts, it feeds itself.

That runaway release explains the rumbling and the surge. The lake dropped by about a metre, releasing an estimated cubic kilometre of carbon dioxide. That's roughly four hundred thousand Olympic swimming pools.

It explains the colour, too. The deep water was rich in iron. When it reached the air, the iron oxidised, and the lake turned rusty red.

What set it off is still unresolved. A landslide, strong wind, or cool rainwater sinking from the surface could each have tipped the balance. A few researchers argue for a pulse of volcanic gas from below. The mechanism is widely agreed. The first push is not.

We breathe out carbon dioxide all the time. It normally makes up about zero point zero four percent of the air. The danger is concentration.

Carbon dioxide is about one and a half times heavier than air, so the cloud didn't drift away. It spilled over the crater rim and flowed downhill, like water. North, through Nyos village. West, along the Cha valley. It stayed lethal up to about ten kilometres from the lake.

At around ten percent carbon dioxide, a person can lose consciousness within minutes. Above about seventeen percent, it can happen within a minute. The cloud arrived at night, when most people were indoors or asleep.

And it lingered. For days afterward, people collapsed at low stream crossings where gas was still pooling.

An eruption announces itself with heat, ash and fire. At Nyos, there was a rumble, a mist, and then silence.

The gas kept coming. Within a few years, it was building up again in the deep water. By the late nineteen-nineties, researchers warned the lake was starting to look the way it had before 1986.

The fix used the same physics. Run a pipe from the deep water to the surface, and pump a little water up to start it. As it rises, gas comes out, the water gets lighter and rises faster, pulling more deep water in behind it. Then it runs on its own.

In 2001, a French-led team installed the first permanent pipe, drawing water from about two hundred metres down. The chain reaction that killed in 1986 was now running through a pipe, slowly, and under control.

One pipe would take decades to clear the lake, so in 2011, two more were added. Scientists have monitored the lake since 1986. And in 2020, members of the French team reported that a single pipe could now keep pace with the carbon dioxide seeping in.

Before Monoun and Nyos, there was no scientific record of a lake doing this. Now it has a name: a limnic eruption. Researchers are watching other lakes, including Lake Kivu, between Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where around two million people live near the shore.

Ephriam Che found Lake Nyos red and silent. Now, a pipe sends a column of water and gas into the air above it.

The carbon dioxide is still seeping up from below, exactly as it did before 1986. The difference is that now, it doesn't get the chance to wait.
